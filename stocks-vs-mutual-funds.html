Stocks vs Mutual Funds

Understand the key differences between individual stocks and mutual funds, compare their risks and potential returns, and learn which investment option may be better suited to your financial goals.

📅 July 2026 ⏱ 16 min read ✍ MoneyGuide Team

One of the first decisions new investors face is whether to invest in individual stocks or mutual funds. Both options can be useful tools for building long-term wealth, but they work in very different ways. The choice between them can influence how much research you need to perform, how diversified your portfolio is, how much volatility you experience, and how actively you need to manage your investments. Individual stocks allow investors to own shares of a specific company. When you buy shares, your investment is directly connected to the performance and future prospects of that business. Mutual funds work differently because they pool money from many investors and use that money to purchase a collection of securities according to the fund's investment strategy. Neither option is automatically better for every investor. A person who enjoys researching businesses and is comfortable with company-specific risk may prefer individual stocks. Someone who wants diversification and professional management may prefer mutual funds. The most important question is not simply which investment can potentially make more money. Instead, investors should consider how each option fits their financial goals, investment timeline, risk tolerance, knowledge, available time, and overall portfolio.

1. What Are Individual Stocks?

Buying an individual stock means purchasing a small ownership interest in a publicly traded company. If you buy shares of a company, the value of those shares can rise or fall depending on market conditions, company performance, investor expectations, economic developments, and many other factors. For example, a company that increases its revenue, improves profitability, expands into new markets, or develops a successful product may attract more investors. Increased demand for its shares can potentially push the stock price higher. However, the opposite can also happen. A company may experience declining sales, increased competition, regulatory challenges, poor management decisions, rising costs, or other problems. In those situations, investors may lose confidence and the stock price may decline. Some companies also pay dividends. A dividend is a distribution that a company may make to shareholders. Investors who own dividend-paying stocks can potentially receive income while also benefiting from changes in the share price. The important point is that owning an individual stock gives you direct exposure to the fortunes of one particular company. That can create significant opportunities, but it also creates company-specific risk.

2. What Are Mutual Funds?

A mutual fund is an investment vehicle that pools money from many investors and uses that money to purchase a portfolio of securities. Depending on the fund's objective, the portfolio may contain stocks, bonds, cash equivalents, or other investments. Instead of researching and buying dozens of individual securities yourself, you can purchase shares of a mutual fund and gain exposure to the investments held inside that fund. Many mutual funds are professionally managed. The fund's management team follows a particular investment strategy and makes decisions about which securities to buy, hold, or sell. For example, one mutual fund might focus on large companies, while another could invest in international stocks, bonds, dividend-paying companies, or a combination of different assets. This structure can make mutual funds appealing to investors who want diversification without having to individually research and monitor a large number of investments.

4. Understanding Investment Risk

Risk is one of the biggest differences between owning individual stocks and investing in mutual funds. However, risk should not be viewed as simply the possibility that an investment will go down. Investment risk can include price volatility, company-specific problems, economic uncertainty, changes in interest rates, inflation, liquidity concerns, and the possibility that an investment will perform differently from what you expected. An individual stock can experience a major decline because of a problem affecting one company. Even if the overall economy and stock market remain relatively healthy, one business can experience financial difficulties. A mutual fund can reduce some of this company-specific risk because it generally owns multiple securities. If one company inside the fund performs poorly, the effect on the total portfolio may be smaller than it would be for someone who owns only that company's stock. However, diversification does not eliminate investment risk. A mutual fund that invests primarily in stocks can still decline significantly during a broad market downturn.

5. Potential Returns

Individual stocks can potentially produce substantial returns when a company performs exceptionally well. An investor who identifies a successful company early may benefit significantly if its earnings, business operations, and market value grow over time. However, the possibility of higher returns comes with the possibility of significant losses. A stock that performs poorly can lose a large percentage of its value, and there is no guarantee that it will recover. Mutual funds generally provide broader exposure to multiple securities. This diversification can make returns less dependent on the performance of one company. A mutual fund may not experience the extraordinary increase that a single highly successful stock can produce. At the same time, it may also be less exposed to the devastating effect of one company's failure. Investors should therefore avoid judging an investment solely by its highest possible return. Risk, consistency, diversification, fees, time horizon, and the probability of achieving a financial goal are also important.

6. Diversification: One of the Biggest Differences

Diversification means spreading investments across multiple companies, industries, geographic regions, or asset classes. The goal is to avoid having too much of your financial future dependent on one investment. When you purchase one individual stock, your investment is concentrated in one company. If that company experiences serious financial problems, your portfolio can be affected dramatically. You can diversify a stock portfolio by purchasing shares of many different companies. However, building and maintaining that portfolio requires more research and ongoing decision-making. Mutual funds provide diversification within a single investment because one fund can hold many different securities. The exact level of diversification depends on the fund's investment strategy. For example, a broad stock mutual fund may hold companies across multiple industries, while a sector-specific mutual fund may concentrate on a narrower part of the market. Diversification can reduce the impact of a single investment performing poorly, but it cannot guarantee profits or prevent losses during broad market declines.

7. How Much Control Do You Want?

Individual stocks provide investors with a high level of control. You decide exactly which companies to buy, how many shares to own, and when to sell. This flexibility can be attractive to experienced investors who have strong opinions about particular companies or industries. However, control also creates responsibility. You need to research businesses, evaluate financial information, monitor company developments, consider valuation, and determine whether a stock still belongs in your portfolio. Mutual funds provide less control over individual securities. When you buy a fund, you generally accept the fund's investment strategy and allow its management team to determine which securities are included. For many investors, this trade-off is worthwhile because they prefer a simpler approach and do not want to spend significant time analyzing individual companies.

8. Active vs Passive Investment Approaches

Another important consideration is whether you prefer an active or passive approach to investing. Some mutual funds are actively managed. In these funds, portfolio managers research investments and make decisions based on the fund's stated strategy. Other funds may follow a specific index or market segment. The goal is generally to provide exposure to the selected market rather than relying on a manager to frequently choose individual securities. Individual stock investing is usually more active from the investor's perspective. You decide which companies to own and must determine when to make changes. Neither approach is automatically superior for every investor. What matters is whether the strategy is appropriate for your goals, risk tolerance, time horizon, and willingness to manage your portfolio.

9. Costs and Fees

Investment costs deserve careful attention because even relatively small fees can affect long-term results. Buying individual stocks may involve trading costs depending on the brokerage and account type. Many modern brokerages offer low-cost or commission-free stock trades, although other costs may still apply. Mutual funds can have expense ratios, management expenses, transaction-related costs, and in some cases sales charges. The exact cost structure varies between funds. An expense ratio represents the annual operating expenses of a fund as a percentage of its assets. Investors should review this information before purchasing a mutual fund. A lower-cost fund is not automatically the best fund, but investors should understand what they are paying and whether the cost is reasonable for the services and strategy provided.

10. Liquidity and Trading Flexibility

Liquidity refers to how easily an investment can be bought or sold. Individual stocks traded on major exchanges can generally be bought or sold during market trading hours, subject to market conditions and brokerage processes. Mutual funds operate differently. Mutual fund transactions are generally processed based on the fund's net asset value according to the fund's trading rules and schedule. For investors who want to make their own decisions about individual securities and trading timing, stocks can provide greater direct control. For investors who prefer a more structured pooled investment, the mutual fund format may be simpler. Understanding how an investment is priced and traded can help you avoid surprises when you need to buy, sell, or rebalance your portfolio.

11. Investment Minimums

The amount of money needed to begin investing can vary considerably depending on the brokerage, fund, share price, and account. Some brokerages allow investors to purchase fractional shares, which can make individual stocks accessible with relatively small amounts of money. Mutual funds can have minimum initial investment requirements, although some funds and investment platforms offer low or no minimums. Before choosing an investment, check the minimum investment requirement and determine whether it fits comfortably within your available investing budget. You should not feel pressured to invest more money than you can reasonably afford. Building a strong financial foundation, maintaining an appropriate emergency fund, and addressing high-priority financial obligations can be important parts of an overall financial plan.

12. Time Commitment and Research

One of the most overlooked differences between stocks and mutual funds is the amount of time required to manage them. Investing in individual companies can require ongoing research. Investors may need to read company reports, follow business developments, understand competitive conditions, monitor industry trends, and review whether the original investment thesis remains valid. Mutual funds can simplify this process because the fund manager or fund strategy handles the selection of securities. This does not mean mutual fund investors should ignore their investments. You should still understand the fund's objective, holdings, fees, historical behavior, and risk profile. The difference is that you are generally not responsible for making every individual security selection decision.

13. Emotional Investing and Market Volatility

Market volatility can test an investor's discipline. Prices can rise quickly during periods of optimism and fall sharply when investors become concerned about economic or company-specific developments. Individual stocks may be particularly difficult emotionally because a single company can experience dramatic price movements. For example, if a stock falls substantially after disappointing business results, an investor may immediately question whether the investment should be sold. Mutual funds can also experience significant declines, particularly when their underlying investments are exposed to broad market risk. However, diversification may make the experience less dependent on one company's news. Regardless of which investment you choose, emotional decision-making can create problems. Selling purely because of short-term fear or buying simply because an investment has recently increased can cause investors to abandon their long-term strategy.

14. Stocks and Mutual Funds for Long-Term Goals

Your investment time horizon should influence how you think about stocks and mutual funds. Long-term goals may allow investors to tolerate more short-term market fluctuations because there is more time for the portfolio to potentially recover from temporary declines. Shorter-term goals require more careful consideration because a major market decline shortly before the money is needed could create a difficult financial situation. The correct investment mix depends on the individual investor. A portfolio intended for a long-term retirement goal may look very different from an account intended for a purchase that will happen within a few years. Before choosing between stocks and mutual funds, identify when you expect to need the money and how much volatility you can realistically tolerate.

15. Stocks vs Mutual Funds for Beginners

Beginners often find mutual funds easier to understand because they provide diversification without requiring the investor to select dozens of individual companies. A new investor can still learn how businesses and markets work while maintaining a more diversified investment approach. Individual stocks can also be useful for learning, but beginners should understand that researching a company and predicting its future performance is difficult. If a beginner decides to purchase individual stocks, keeping the position appropriately sized within the overall portfolio can help prevent one company from dominating the investor's financial outcome. The most important lesson for beginners is that investing is not a competition to find the next stock that will increase dramatically. Building a sustainable financial plan is generally more important than trying to predict short-term market winners.

16. Who Should Choose Individual Stocks?

  • Investors willing to research individual companies.
  • People comfortable with higher company-specific risk.
  • Experienced investors who understand financial statements.
  • Investors who want direct control over individual holdings.
  • People willing to monitor their investments regularly.
  • Long-term investors who understand that large price declines are possible.
  • Investors who can maintain discipline instead of making emotional decisions.

17. Who Should Choose Mutual Funds?

  • Beginning investors who want a simpler approach.
  • People who prefer instant diversification.
  • Investors who do not want to research individual companies constantly.
  • People who prefer professional portfolio management.
  • Long-term investors building diversified portfolios.
  • Investors who want exposure to a broader market or specific investment category.
  • Individuals who prefer a structured investment strategy.

18. How to Decide Between Stocks and Mutual Funds

Choosing between stocks and mutual funds becomes easier when you evaluate your personal circumstances rather than focusing only on potential returns. Start by asking how much time you want to spend managing your investments. If you enjoy researching businesses and analyzing financial information, individual stocks may be appealing. If you prefer a simpler approach, mutual funds may be more convenient. Next, consider diversification. If you have limited capital and want exposure to many companies, a diversified mutual fund may provide a practical way to achieve that exposure. Risk tolerance is equally important. Consider how you would react if an investment declined significantly. If watching one stock fall sharply would cause you to panic and sell, concentrating too much money in individual stocks may not be appropriate. Finally, consider your investment goal. A portfolio designed for long-term wealth building may have different requirements from one designed for a shorter-term objective.

19. Can You Own Both Stocks and Mutual Funds?

Yes. Investors do not necessarily have to choose only one option. Some portfolios use mutual funds as a diversified foundation while also holding a smaller selection of individual stocks. This approach can provide broad market exposure while allowing the investor to express specific views about companies they understand well. For example, an investor may use diversified funds for most of their portfolio and reserve a smaller portion for individual companies. However, combining investments does not automatically make a portfolio diversified. You should examine the underlying holdings of your mutual funds and stocks to understand whether you are accidentally concentrating your portfolio in the same companies or industries.

20. Rebalancing Your Portfolio

Portfolio allocation can change over time because different investments experience different returns. Suppose individual stocks rise significantly while mutual funds remain relatively stable. The stocks could eventually represent a much larger portion of the portfolio than you originally intended. Rebalancing involves reviewing your portfolio and adjusting holdings to return toward your desired allocation. The appropriate frequency for reviewing or rebalancing a portfolio depends on your investment strategy and circumstances. The important principle is to have a plan rather than making changes solely because the market has recently moved.

21. Common Mistakes to Avoid

Many new investors purchase individual stocks based on social media trends, headlines, or recommendations without understanding the underlying business. This can lead to decisions based on excitement rather than careful analysis. Another common mistake is putting too much money into a single company. Even if you strongly believe in a business, unexpected events can affect its performance. Mutual fund investors can make mistakes too. Some investors choose funds based solely on recent performance, without examining fees, investment strategy, portfolio composition, or risk. Another mistake is ignoring costs. A fund with higher expenses must generally overcome those costs before investors receive the corresponding benefit. Investors should also avoid constantly changing strategies based on short-term market movements. A long-term plan can become ineffective if it is repeatedly abandoned during periods of uncertainty.

  • Buying stocks solely because they are trending.
  • Concentrating too much money in one company.
  • Ignoring mutual fund expenses.
  • Choosing funds based only on recent performance.
  • Trading emotionally during market declines.
  • Failing to consider your investment time horizon.
  • Ignoring diversification.
  • Investing without a clear financial objective.

22. A Simple Example

Imagine two investors who each have the same amount of money to invest. Investor A uses the money to purchase shares of one company. If that company performs extremely well, the investor could potentially experience strong growth. However, if the company encounters serious problems, the investment could decline substantially. Investor B places the money into a diversified mutual fund holding many different companies. If one company inside the fund performs poorly, the impact may be limited because the fund owns other securities as well. This example does not mean that the mutual fund will always outperform the individual stock. The single stock could outperform the fund by a large margin. The point is that the two investors are accepting different types and levels of concentration risk. Understanding that difference is more useful than simply asking which investment has the higher possible return.

23. Questions to Ask Before Investing

Before purchasing an individual stock or mutual fund, take some time to understand what you are actually buying. Consider the following questions:

  • What is the purpose of this investment?
  • How long do I expect to hold it?
  • How much loss could I realistically tolerate?
  • How diversified is my overall portfolio?
  • What companies or assets am I actually exposed to?
  • What fees and expenses will I pay?
  • How much research and monitoring will this investment require?
  • Would I still be comfortable owning it during a major market decline?
  • Does this investment support my broader financial plan?

These questions can help shift your focus away from short-term market excitement and toward the factors that matter most for long-term investing.

24. Stocks vs Mutual Funds: Which Is Better?

There is no universal answer to whether stocks or mutual funds are better. Individual stocks may be appropriate for investors who want direct ownership, greater control, and the possibility of significant growth from specific companies. However, they require investors to accept more company-specific risk and take responsibility for researching and monitoring individual businesses. Mutual funds may be better suited to investors who value diversification, professional management, and a simpler investment process. They can make it easier to spread money across multiple securities, although investors should still evaluate fees, strategy, holdings, and risk. The best option is the one that fits your financial goals and that you can realistically stick with through different market environments.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are stocks better than mutual funds?

Not necessarily. Stocks can offer higher potential returns but also carry greater company-specific risk. Mutual funds provide diversification and can be easier to manage, especially for investors who do not want to research individual companies.

Can I invest in both stocks and mutual funds?

Yes. Many investors combine diversified funds with selected individual stocks. The important consideration is how the combined investments affect the risk and diversification of the entire portfolio.

Which option is better for beginners?

Mutual funds can be easier for beginners because they provide diversification and can reduce the need to select and monitor many individual companies. However, beginners can also learn about individual stocks if they understand the additional risks and responsibilities involved.

Do mutual funds guarantee profits?

No. Mutual funds are investments and their values can rise or fall. Diversification can reduce some company-specific risk, but it cannot eliminate market risk or guarantee profits.

Can an individual stock outperform a mutual fund?

Yes. A successful individual company can outperform a diversified mutual fund by a significant amount. However, identifying future winners is difficult, and the same concentration that creates greater upside can also create greater downside risk.

Are mutual funds diversified?

Many mutual funds are diversified, but the degree of diversification varies. Some funds invest across hundreds of companies, while others focus on a particular industry, geographic region, or asset category. Always review the fund's holdings and strategy.

Do stocks or mutual funds have lower fees?

The answer depends on the specific investment and brokerage. Individual stock investing may have low trading costs, while mutual funds can charge expense ratios and other expenses. Compare the total costs before investing.

Should I invest in stocks for short-term goals?

Investments with significant market volatility can create risks when money is needed soon. Before investing for a short-term goal, consider whether a market decline could interfere with your ability to pay for that goal.

How many stocks should I own to be diversified?

There is no single number that guarantees diversification. Diversification depends on the number of holdings as well as their industries, sizes, geographic exposure, and correlations. A broadly diversified mutual fund can provide diversification without requiring an investor to individually select many companies.

Conclusion

Both individual stocks and mutual funds can play valuable roles in a long-term investment strategy. The important difference is how much control, diversification, research responsibility, and company-specific risk each option places on the investor. Individual stocks give you direct ownership of specific businesses. They can provide substantial growth potential when a company performs exceptionally well, but they also expose you to the possibility that one company's problems could significantly affect your portfolio. Mutual funds provide a different approach. By pooling investments across multiple securities, they can offer diversification and reduce the impact of any single company. Many mutual funds also provide professional management or follow a defined investment strategy, which can make them easier to manage. Neither investment type guarantees profits, and neither eliminates risk. A diversified mutual fund can decline during a broad market downturn, while an individual stock can potentially deliver strong returns or substantial losses. The right decision should therefore begin with your financial situation rather than with a prediction about which investment will perform best next year.

Before investing, think about your goals, time horizon, risk tolerance, knowledge, available time, and desired level of control. Consider how much volatility you could tolerate without abandoning your strategy and whether you are willing to research individual businesses. If you prefer direct control and enjoy analyzing companies, individual stocks may have a place in your portfolio. If you prefer diversification and a more hands-off approach, mutual funds may be more appropriate. You can also combine the two approaches if doing so fits your overall investment plan. A diversified fund can provide broad exposure while individual stocks can provide more targeted exposure to companies you understand.

Ultimately, successful investing is less about finding one perfect investment and more about creating a strategy you can maintain over many years. Diversification, reasonable costs, consistent investing, appropriate risk management, and a long-term perspective can all contribute to a stronger investment process. Do not choose an investment simply because it has recently performed well. Instead, understand what you own, why you own it, what risks it carries, and how it fits into your larger financial goals. The best investment strategy is one that you understand, can afford to maintain, and can continue following when markets become uncertain.
